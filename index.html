THE PROGRAMAGES 12–14CORE · ADVANCED · FOUNDER CHALLENGE

The destination is ownership, not a business plan for its own sake.

Youth entrepreneurship programs mostly end where they begin: a product idea, a poster, a sale. Young Owners teaches what they leave out, in order, and treats every scholar as capable.

Self→Problem→Customer→Solution→Business→Money→Technology→Media→IP→Ownership→Venture→Impact

Real customers, real money

Scholars interview customers, build and test a minimum viable product, set a price, project profit and cash flow, and keep a Venture Portfolio that grows by one page every chapter.

Technology, AI and media

Four chapters on technology, artificial intelligence, automation and AI-assisted ventures, with a standing rule: AI can be wrong, can repeat bias and can fabricate. Six more on creativity, media, storytelling, audience, intellectual property and the creator company.

History and economic power

A volume on Black enterprise, economic power, community entrepreneurship and generational assets, built from verifiable case studies, historical and contemporary, and taught as achievement and agency.

THE FOUNDER INDEX

Measured at the start and the end.

Ten capabilities, ten points each: problem solving, business knowledge, financial intelligence, communication, creativity, technology and AI, leadership, research, execution, ownership mindset. The scholar scores, the educator scores, the two are averaged. It measures growth, not rank: nobody is compared with anyone else.

A LOOK INSIDECHAPTER 01 · KNOW YOURSELF

Six scholars carry the book. Chapter 01 opens at a kitchen table.

They disagree, test ideas, make mistakes and revise — and they help a reader with the page. Every chapter is written to the scholar, in the book's own comic register.

Chapter 01 · WHY IT MATTERS — Dixon and Donavonne sat at a kitchen table, two notebooks open, arguing about what to build first.

I'm starting the AI tutoring thing. I already do it every Thursday with the coding club. I have a laptop. I have the software. I spend zero dollars to start.

Cool. But I already have five pairs of used basketball shoes in my garage. I cleaned them, took photos, posted them… and sold three pairs yesterday for $36. That's real money. Real. Money.

You're both missing the point. You picked ventures that match what you already do. The question isn't which venture makes more money. It's which one you can actually start today with what you already have.

That is why self-awareness matters. Owners who know themselves avoid ventures that drain them and pursue ventures that use their natural strengths. — Chapter 01, Know Yourself

TEACHER EDITIONFor educators — contains answer keys

Written for the facilitator as much as the teacher.

You do not need a business background to teach this course. The Teacher Edition gives every chapter a minute-by-minute pacing plan, objectives, standards alignment, a teaching card for all thirteen sections, differentiation for English learners and students with IEPs or 504 plans, misconceptions to watch, rubrics, safety and privacy notes, a family letter, vocabulary and the answer key.

Every section has a teaching card
THE NUMBERSCORE8 minIndividualDay 1
WHY
Ground the chapter in real data about teen attitudes toward entrepreneurship.
DO
Read the statistics aloud. Ask students to work individually on the arithmetic: 69 − 66 = 3 (the difference between 2017 and 2020 survey results). Ask: “What does it mean that 66% of teens are interested in starting a business, but 29% think it's ‘too risky’?”
ASK
  1. “The 2017 and 2020 surveys measured different groups of teens, not the same group tracked over time. Why does that matter?”Listen for separate samples; a 3-point gap is not a trend.
  2. “If 13% of current business owners started at 18 or younger, what does that tell you about starting young?”Listen for starting young is not unusual.
CHECK
Students note that the two figures come from separate surveys, and that 13% shows starting young is not unusual.
